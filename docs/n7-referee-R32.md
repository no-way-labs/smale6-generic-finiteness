## A. The three new proofs

### Lemmas 4.1 and 5.2

**Lemma 4.1.** The zero sum allows subtraction of $u_0$. A nonzero integer vector of zero sum has largest nonzero index $p\geq 1$. For $p=1$, the shifted expression is exactly $q_1(u_1-u_0)$, with $u_1-u_0>0$. For $p\geq 2$, the shifted weights are positive and increasing. The threshold bounds the absolute contribution below $p$ by $(1+\tau)|q_p|(u_{p-1}-u_0)$, strictly less than $|q_p|(u_p-u_0)$. The strictness still holds at equality in the threshold on $\tau$. This proves the sign as well as nonvanishing.

**Lemma 5.2, Step 1.** Subtracting the two equations in (5.1) gives $\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_i=2(\mathbf 1_{g_i}-\mathbf 1_{f_i})\cdot u$. Their sum vanishes by $\alpha_r=\alpha_0$. Each edge contributes two incidences, so $\sum_kq_k=0$. Each body has at most $n-1$ incidences in the entire list of distinct pairs. Taking their signed sum gives $|q_k|\leq n-1$. If $q\ne 0$, the lower-index sum in Lemma 4.1 has $p-1\leq n-2$ terms and $|q_p|\geq 1$. The stated threshold suffices and forces $q=0$. In particular, $r=1$ would give $\mathbf 1_{g_0}=\mathbf 1_{f_0}$, impossible for distinct unordered pairs.

**Step 2.** The recurrence gives $\alpha_i=\alpha_0+2\sum_k a_k(i)u_k$. This includes $i=r$ because all $a_k(r)=0$. In the first equation of (5.1), $\mathbf 1_{f_j}(k)=a_k(j)-b_k(j)$ gives the stated formula for $\beta_j$ and hence (5.2). Each $a(i)$ has coordinate sum zero, each $b(j)$ has sum $-2$, and unused bodies contribute zero. For the coefficient bound, subtract the two prefixes defining $a_k(i)$ and $a_k(j)$. The uncancelled summands count signed incidences in the interval between $i$ and $j$, up to an overall sign. Its absolute value is at most $n-1$ by distinctness. Passing from $a_k(j)$ to $b_k(j)$ changes it by at most one.

**Step 3.** The largest participating index $L$ occurs in both signs by Step 1. On the cyclic list alternating $a$ and $b$, decreases occur only from $a_L(i)$ to $b_L(i)$ at incidences of $L$ in $f_i$, and increases occur only from $b_L(j)$ to $a_L(j+1)$ at incidences in $g_j$. Each nonzero step has size one. The minimum set is nonempty and proper because there is a decrease. The circle therefore has an entry to and an exit from that set, giving (5.3), including a plateau or a wrap from $b_L(r-1)$ to $a_L(0)=a_L(r)$. The entry and exit may meet at one position.

**Step 4.** The partners $x$ and $x'$ of $L$ are distinct by edge distinctness and both are below $L$. The heights used for $f_i$ are $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$ and those for $g_j$ are $(\alpha_{j+1},\beta_j)$, with $\alpha_r=\alpha_0$. The sum of the two colourwise minima is at most $\zeta_{xx'}+\omega_{xx'}$, which is at most $2(\Psi^\circ+u_x+u_{x'})$ by maximality over **all** body pairs. Taking one minimizer in each colour gives exactly the four alternatives, with ties or coincident subscripts allowed. The choices $(i,i)$ and $(j+1,j)$ reduce respectively to $u_L\leq u_{x'}$ and $u_L\leq u_x$. For each mixed choice, (5.2) gives $\nu\cdot u\geq 0$. The two positive unit vectors have total coefficient sum $2$, which cancels $\sum_k(a_k(c)-b_k(d))=2$. All indices above $L$ vanish. At $L$ the difference of levels is $(\mu+1)-\mu=1$, so $\nu_L=-1$. Each coordinate has absolute value at most $n+1$. The lower-index sum is at most $(L-1)(n+1)\leq (n-2)(n+1)$, and Lemma 4.1 gives the opposite strict sign. The proof uses the equalities of **heights**, distinct edges and maximality. No equality or distinctness of nodes is required.

**Corollary 5.3 and Example 5.4.** A simple multigraph cycle provides the alternating height equalities by Lemma 3.2(a), including a pair of parallel edges. For the example the height pairs in order $01,02,03,12,13,23$ are $(0,0),(0,2),(0,2),(2,0),(2,0),(2,2)$, giving $\Psi=0$ throughout. With $f_0=01,g_0=12,f_1=23,g_1=03$, the successive shared nodes are the full $w$ root, the $z$ node $\{1,2,3\}$, the $w$ node $\{0,2,3\}$ and the full $z$ root. Also, $02$ and $03$ alone give an $r=1$ cycle at these weights.

**Independent exact search.** I used one integer vector in $W_n(n^2)$ per $n=2,\ldots,6$, taking $u_0=0$ and $u_j=(n^2+2)^{j-1}$ for $j\geq 1$. For each colour, the relations $d_{ab}\geq h$ as $h$ passes the distinct heights form a strictly coarsening chain of partitions from singletons to a single block. The first merge level of each pair specifies its relative height. Enumerating all such chains covers unresolved nodes and ties between unrelated nodes. Exchanging the two colours preserves existence, so I examined each pair of order types once.

For a fixed pair of order types I built the bipartite multigraph on **height ranks**. A closed alternating sequence is an edge-distinct closed walk there, and yields a simple cycle, including length two. I removed a body pair if another pair dominates its two height ranks and mass cost, with at least one comparison strict. For each surviving simple cycle, equality of its terms requires an alternating sum of the integer costs $2(u_a+u_b)$ to vanish. For every candidate passing that check I tested feasibility against all body pairs, with real heights allowed within the specified order types.

For that last test put $x_i=\zeta_i$ and $y_j=2\Psi^\circ-\omega_j$ at the respective ranks. Every pair $(a,b)$ gives $x_i-y_j\leq 2(u_a+u_b)$, with the reverse bound for a cycle edge. Strict rank comparisons are difference bounds of cost zero. For $V$ variables, multiplying all costs by $V+1$ and changing each strict zero cost to $-1$ preserves feasibility. Any simple directed cycle has at most $V$ arcs and its unscaled cost is integral. An originally positive cycle retains positive cost, and a zero-cost cycle becomes negative exactly when it contains a strict comparison. The resulting difference bounds were tested by integer relaxation. All weights, comparisons and feasibility decisions used integers.

### Lemma 2.4, Lemma 8.1 and Theorems 1.2 and 1.1

**Lemma 2.4.** For an irreducible component $V$, the image of $\mathbb G_m\times V$ under the action has irreducible closure and contains $V$ by the identity element. Maximality of $V$ makes that closure $V$. Each orbit has dimension one. The coordinate $X_{01}$ on an orbit recovers the rotation parameter up to a fixed nonzero factor and gives an isomorphism of the orbit with $\mathbb G_m$. Thus there are no zero-dimensional components. An orbit in a one-dimensional component is constructible by Chevalley and dense by dimension, so it contains an open subset of that component. Two distinct orbits in it would give two disjoint nonempty open subsets. The other direction uses that the closure of a finite union of the one-dimensional orbits has dimension at most one.

**Lemma 8.1, fibre and constructibility.** Write $s=\ker(R\to k)$ for the point below $m$. The map $R\to k$ factors through $\kappa(s)=\operatorname{Frac}(R/s)$. Substituting the masses in the polynomial quotient is tensoring with $k$ over $R$, which gives $\mathcal X_s\times_{\operatorname{Spec}\kappa(s)}\operatorname{Spec}k$. The topological dimensions and the set of $k$-points are unaffected by nilpotents. All four cited tags apply.

- Tag 02FZ applies to a locally finite type morphism and gives openness of the locus where the fibre dimension at the point is at most one. It is this dimension, the maximum dimension of a component through the point, that is used in $\mathcal X_{\ge 2}$. [Stacks, 02FZ](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/02FZ).
- Tag 054K assumes quasi-compactness and local finite presentation and takes a locally constructible subset to a locally constructible image. The morphism here is affine of finite presentation. Its source and target are affine Noetherian schemes, so the closed source locus and the locally constructible target image are constructible. [Stacks, 054K](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/054K).
- Tag 02FY(1) preserves the dimension of the fibre at each point above a given point under base change. Part (3) provides a point above each original point, or use faithful flatness of a field extension. For part (a), the existence of such a point is equivalent to existence of an irreducible component of dimension at least two. Over algebraically closed $k$, the closed points of the reduced finite type affine fibre are exactly the classical solution set, and are dense in each component. The irreducible components and their dimensions correspond to those of that set by the Nullstellensatz. Lemma 2.4 applies to this set. [Stacks, 02FY](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/02FY).
- Tag 005K gives that a dense constructible subset contains the generic point of an irreducible space that has one. Here $R$ is a domain. For part (b), rational linear independence makes all monomial exponents distinct in a nonzero polynomial evaluated at the proposed monomials. The resulting Hahn series is nonzero, so the evaluation kernel really is $(0)$. [Stacks, 005K](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/005K).

Poonen's Theorem 1 gives maximal completeness of the equal-characteristic Mal'cev–Neumann field. Corollary 4 gives algebraic closure when the value group is divisible and the residue field is algebraically closed. These are $\mathbb R$ and $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ here. For any real $\gamma$, the distinct $cT^\gamma$ for $c\in\overline{\mathbb Q}^{*}$ give infinitely many elements of valuation $\gamma$. [Poonen, Theorem 1 and Corollary 4](https://math.mit.edu/~poonen/papers/amsval.pdf#page=10).

**Theorems 1.2 and 1.1.** The chamber is nonempty and open, so it meets the complement of the countable union of rational linear hyperplanes. Theorem 1.3 and Lemma 8.1 then exclude the generic point from $D$ and imply $\overline D\subsetneq\operatorname{Spec}R$. Write $\overline D=V(I)$ and choose $0\ne H\in I$. For complex $m$ with infinitely many orbits, $H$ is in the kernel of $R\to\mathbb C$ by Lemma 8.1(a). Hence $H(m)=0$. The zero locus is proper over $\mathbb C$ because the coefficient map $\mathbb Q\to\mathbb C$ is injective and $\mathbb C$ is infinite. One may take $H=1$ if $D$ is empty. A nonzero polynomial also cannot vanish on the entire open positive real orthant. The resulting $\Sigma_n$ has the stated properties, and Lemmas 2.1–2.2 give the physical conclusion.

### Proposition 7.1 and Theorem 1.3

1. A regular function on an irreducible component has irreducible image closure and constructible image. In the affine line over an algebraically closed field, a nonconstant such function has cofinite image. A cofinite set meets every valuation class since each class has infinitely many elements. Taking valuation $M+1$ gives the contradiction. Each resulting constant $\delta_{tl}$ is nonzero.
2. The equation $P_{tl}^2=\delta_{tl}^{-1}$ confines $P_{tl}$ to a finite set. Irreducibility makes it constant.
3. The inverse to the slice parametrization is $p\mapsto (X_{ab}(p),X_{ab}(p)^{-1}\cdot p)$, where the dot is the rotation. It is regular because $X_{ab}$ is a unit, also directly by (1.2). This gives the claimed dimension drop by an isomorphism. Adding the inverse to the text would justify the dimension statement immediately.
4. On the slice $Y_{ab}=\delta_{ab}$. Subtracting the two equations involving a third body $c$ gives $Y_{ca}=\delta_{cb}-\delta_{ca}-\delta_{ab}-\delta_{ab}X_{ca}$. The substitution gives exactly the printed quadratic with leading coefficient $-\delta_{ab}\ne0$. There are finitely many choices for all differences because each is determined by differences based at $a$. For $n=2$ the chosen $ab$ differences are already all of them. The equations for $X_t,Y_t$ give the positions from the differences and the fixed $m,P$, including for total mass zero. Thus $V_1$ is finite, contradicting positive dimension.

The bound applied in Theorem 1.3 is $M=\frac23(2n-1)\sum_j |u_j|+4\max_j|u_j|$. It follows from Corollary 6.3 by bounding $2u_t+2u_l$ and depends only on the fixed mass vector through $u$. Theorem 1.3 follows from Proposition 7.1 and Lemma 2.4.

## B. The remaining proof steps

### Lemmas 2.1–2.3

Summing the conjugate-weighted central configuration equations gives $\lambda I=U$, since the numerator for a pair is $(z_t-z_l)(\overline{z_t-c}-\overline{z_l-c})=|z_t-z_l|^2$. Positivity makes $I,U>0$, hence $\lambda>0$, and makes the total mass positive when summing the force equations to identify $c$. Translating by $-c$ and scaling by $\lambda^{1/3}$ gives the normalization. An affine similarity between normalized configurations fixes the centre and has scale one. Its linear part is a rotation or a rotation composed with reflection.

For normalized real configurations (viewed in $\mathbb C$), the conjugate coordinates and positive $P$ satisfy the equations. Real masses suffice for the conjugation step of Lemma 2.2(a). If two such solutions are in the same complex orbit, $s z_t=\bar s^{-1}z_t$ for all $t$ and at least one $z_t$ is nonzero. Thus $|s|=1$. Finitely many complex orbits give finitely many normalized physical classes modulo rotations and therefore finitely many modulo similarities. Lemma 2.3 uses (1.2) for all nonzero differences and $P$ values, and $sX_{01}=X_{01}$ for freeness. Positivity supplies the physical normalization. The complex and Hahn-series proof steps use the stated algebraic and valuation hypotheses.

### Section 3

**Lemma 3.2.** In (a), the balls at height $h=d_{xy}$ centred at $x$ and $y$ are equal by the ultrametric inequality. All internal distances are at least $h$. A second generating pair for the same node gives both inequalities between the two heights. In (b) the relation $d_{pp'}>h$ is transitive, and distinct children have distance exactly $h$. In (c) the inequality with $p$ as the intermediary would put $p'$ in $A$ if $d_{pp'}\geq h$.

The invocation of (a) in (d) requires a repair. The printed sentence begins “By (a) applied to the pairs $\{x,p\}$ and $\{p,x\}$”. That pair can have $p=x$ or $d_{xp}=+\infty$. Even with a finite $d_{xp}>h$, part (a) as invoked equates balls of radius threshold $d_{xp}$. Replace the invocation with the fixed-threshold argument. Since $d_{xp}\geq h$, one has $d_{xq}\geq h$ if and only if $d_{pq}\geq h$ by applying the ultrametric inequality in both directions. With $d_{pp'}=h$ this is exactly $[p,p']_d=A$, also for repeated indices in the inequalities using the infinite diagonal.

**Definition 3.3 and Lemma 3.4.** In a $z$ cut the term $(t,O)$ occurs exactly when $\xi_t$ is finite, independently of $\eta_t$. Thus it can have a finite cut value even if $\Psi(t,O)=-\infty$. The $w$ cut uses the analogous test on $\eta_t$. Cancelling internal pairs in the mass-weighted sum leaves one term for each crossing body pair. Its valuation is $u_t+u_l-\frac12\zeta_{tl}-\frac32\omega_{tl}=-\Phi^z(t,l)$. The origin term has valuation $u_t+\xi_t=-\Phi^z(t,O)$ when it is present. A nonempty proper cut has a nonzero crossing force term. The full cut has a nonzero position term since the $X_t$ are distinct and $n\geq2$. The least valuation is attained twice because the sum is zero. Exchanging $X$ and $Y$ gives the other cut.

**Example 3.5.** Subtracting the two $X$ equations gives $P_{01}^3(m_0+m_1)=1$ since $X_{01}\ne0$. When $u_0<u_1$, $v(m_0+m_1)=u_0$, so (1.2) gives $\zeta_{01}+\omega_{01}=2u_0/3$. The individual force equations give $\xi_1=\zeta_{01}$ and $\xi_0=\zeta_{01}+u_1-u_0$, and the corresponding formulas for $\eta$. Thus $\Psi(0,1)=\Psi(1,O)=-(2u_0+3u_1)/3$ and $\Psi(0,O)=\Psi(1,O)-(u_1-u_0)$. Both maximizers meet the root node in each colour.

### Theorem 6.2 and Corollary 6.3

1. For an edge $e$ of $E^*$ let $A$ be its $w$ node and $h$ its finite height. Its endpoints lie in different children, so one of those children $S$ omits $O$. If an endpoint is $O$, take the child containing the body. For every cut term $g=\{p,p'\}$ with $p\in S$ and $p'\notin S$, Lemma 3.2(b)–(c) gives $\omega_g\le h$, with equality precisely for $p'\in A$ across children. In particular if $Y_p=0$, then $\omega_{pO}=+\infty>h$ would place $O$ in $A$ and alongside $p$ in its child. Such $p$ cannot lie in $S$. An $O$ term of the $z$ cut thus has both heights finite and obeys $\Phi^z(g)=\Psi(g)+\omega_g$. Now $\Phi^z(g)\leq\Psi^*+h$, and equality forces membership in $E^*$ and the same $w$ node by Lemma 3.2(d). The edge $e$ is one such term, including when it ends at $O$ since finiteness of $\Psi(e)$ ensures $\xi_x$ is real. The cut condition provides another. Exchange the colours for the $z$ node. The longest-path argument gives a cycle in the finite bipartite multigraph, also when the returning edge is parallel to the last path edge.
2. For **any** closed alternating sequence, the height equalities give $\sum_e\varepsilon(e)\omega_e=\sum_e\varepsilon(e)\zeta_e=0$. Substituting from $\Psi$ gives $(2\sigma-\sigma_O)\Psi^*+q\cdot u=0$. The signs give $\sigma_O=-\sigma$, hence $N=3\sigma$. Each signed body pair contributes $4\varepsilon(e)$ to the coordinate sum of $q$, and each signed $O$-edge contributes $-\varepsilon(e)$. The sum is $5\sigma=5N/3$.
3. If the sequence uses only body pairs, one of them attains $\Psi^*$, so $\Psi^\circ=\Psi^*$. Restricting both ultrametrics to the bodies preserves the height equalities and keeps those pairs in $E^\circ$. Lemma 5.2 applies directly to the closed alternating sequence.
4. An $O$-edge through $t$ occurs once and makes $q_t$ odd, as all other contributions there are even. If $N=0$, Step 2 gives $\sum_j q_j=0$ and $q\cdot u=0$. Each body has at most $n-1$ distinct body pairs and one $O$-edge in the list. Thus $|q_j|\leq2n-1$ and the lower-index sum is at most $(n-2)(2n-1)$ with $|q_p|\geq1$. This is within the threshold of Lemma 4.1 and gives a contradiction.
5. A cycle exists, and for every cycle $|N|\geq3$ and the incidence bound of Step 4 holds. Then $\Psi^*=-q\cdot u/N\leq\frac13(2n-1)\sum_j|u_j|$. The pairs $(N,q)$ may be taken from the finite set obtained by assigning alternating signs to **all lists of distinct edges** on $n+1$ vertices of positive even length. This set depends only on $n$, even though the sequences that alternate in height depend on the ultrametrics. Corollary 6.3 follows by applying the bound to $2\Psi(t,l)+2u_t+2u_l$. The signs of Example 6.4 are $N=-3$, $q=(-2,-3)$ as stated.

## C. Statements, scope and literature

### Statement-only pass

The statements and definitions give the proof chain. Lemmas 2.1–2.4 relate the physical problem to the dimension of the algebraic solution set. Definitions 3.1 and 3.3 and Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4 provide the cut data. Lemmas 4.1 and 5.2 supply the separation and cycle exclusion used in Theorem 6.2. Corollary 6.3 and Proposition 7.1 give Theorem 1.3, and Lemma 8.1 transfers the conclusion to generic masses. The three introductory theorems state their mass and field hypotheses correctly. The following changes would make the individual statements easier to use.

- Fix the convention for similarity where it first appears, including whether reflections are allowed. On the current convention of Lemma 2.1 the three-body count in the introduction requires the correction below. Restrict the comment about $|s|=1$ to $k=\mathbb C$.
- Put the two disjoint sets of coloured vertices of $\Gamma(E)$ into its definition. Say explicitly that a simple cycle in this multigraph may consist of two distinct parallel edges. This case is needed in Example 6.4. Put the body-only scope of $E^\circ$ next to Lemma 5.2 if it will be read in isolation.
- Include “of $\mathcal N_n$ over $K$ at $m$” in Proposition 7.1's statement. Lemma 8.1 is readable with its preceding definition of $\mathcal M$ and “point below $m$”. Use “fibre dimension at $x$” consistently there because the dimension of the local ring at $x$ has a different meaning.
- In Remark 4.2 say “extend the formula (1.5) to negative $\tau$” or use the inequalities directly. The remark otherwise uses the notation outside its declared domain. Its example is numerically correct.

No proof introduces an unstated mathematical hypothesis. Some stated assumptions can be weakened. The proof of Lemma 4.1 works with real $q$ under its displayed threshold, and integrality is used in the applications for $|q_p|\geq 1$ and parity. The proof of Theorem 6.2(a) uses the weight datum alone. Part (b) uses the $n^2$ threshold of Lemma 5.2. Proposition 7.1 works with any fixed masses because its rigidity argument uses the position equations without dividing by a mass or total mass. In the proof of Lemma 2.2, reality is used for conjugation and positivity supplies normalization through Lemma 2.1. The weights, nodes and $\Psi$ retain consistent meanings throughout the proof.

### Abstract, introduction and Remark 1.4

- The statement of the all-positive-mass question follows Smale's Problem 6, where he also credits Wintner. The three numbered results in the draft give the advertised generic physical, generic complex and Hahn-series scopes. [Smale, Problem 6](https://www.fim.uni-passau.de/fileadmin/dokumente/fakultaeten/fim/lehrstuhl/muller/SmaleProblems1998.pdf).
- The five three-body classes are counted modulo orientation-preserving similarities. The two labelled Lagrange triangles become one class when reflections are allowed, giving four classes with the equivalence in Lemma 2.1. Add “orientation-preserving” specifically to the historical count or give the four-class count. [Hampton and Moeckel, §1](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~rmoeckel/research/FinitenessRE4BP.rev.pdf).
- Qualify “A continuum of solutions would contain collisions of every order” in the abstract and the analogous sentence in Method by **modulo rotation**. Every solution already supplies a one-dimensional orbit of constant $X_{tl}Y_{tl}$. The proved statement is that on any irreducible component of dimension at least two, some $X_{tl}Y_{tl}$ takes values of every real valuation. All body differences stay nonzero.
- The abstract and the formal-proof paragraph ascribe entire results to Lean where Table 4 gives more limited statements. Name the weight-datum assertion of Lemma 3.4, the nonvanishing assertion of Lemma 4.1, the simple-cycle results and the collision bound in the summary. See Part D.
- Replace the broad methodological description of the earlier proofs by a description of the diagram approach of Albouy–Kaloshin and the six-body preprints. In particular, the Jensen–Leykin calculation uses recession cones of a tropical prevariety. The two six-body preprints prove or claim their results for the physical positive configurations. [Jensen and Leykin, §4](https://jexpmath.org/index.php/jem/article/download/Vol-1Issue-2Paper-6/Vol-1Issue-2Paper-6/64903), [Tomar, proof of Theorem 1.1](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00377v1#page=29), [Chen and Tong, Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.23329v1).
- The scope of Remark 1.4 is correct. For an independent check of the positive-mass complex continuum, take $a,b>0$ with $a^2+b^2=1$ and the four outer positions $2a,2ib,-2a,-2ib$ around $0$. Use conjugate $Y$, positive inverse distances for outer pairs and negative inverse distances for the four centre pairs. Each side has $P=1/2$. The centre inverse distance for each outer body is $-2$ times its opposite-pair inverse distance, so the diagonal and centre forces cancel at that body for masses $(4,4,4,4,1)$. The two side forces sum to its position. The forces cancel at the centre by symmetry. The squared distance $16a^2$ for one diagonal varies, giving infinitely many complex-rotation orbits. Their centre-pair root signs exclude them from the physical normalization. Hence every permissible $\Sigma_5$ for Theorem 1.2 contains this vector. For Theorem 1.1 the all-positive-mass question is equivalent to being able to choose an exceptional set disjoint from the positive orthant. [Albouy and Kaloshin, §2 p. 543](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v176-n1-p10-p.pdf#page=9).
- A coarse uniform count is available directly from the proof. On $X_{01}=1$ there is one point per orbit and one variable can be eliminated. The remaining equations have degree at most four in $2n+\binom n2-1$ variables. When the slice is finite, the affine Bézout bound on isolated points gives at most $4^{2n+\binom n2-1}$ orbits. The abstract and Remark 1.4 can say that no sharp enumeration is attempted, and Problem 12.1 can ask for a sharper bound.
